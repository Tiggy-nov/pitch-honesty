# Synthetic Example

This example is fictional.

## Input
**Company:** ClaimPilot  
**Product:** AI agent for commercial insurance claims.  
**Customer:** Mid-market insurers.  
**Current workflow:** Adjusters reconcile emails, PDFs, photos, policy documents, and repair estimates across multiple systems.  
**Claimed result:** Six hours of administrative work reduced to 35 minutes.  
**Traction:** Three paid pilots; one moved to production.

## Weak founder description
> ClaimPilot is an AI-powered end-to-end claims automation platform leveraging multimodal agents to transform the insurance ecosystem.

## Pass 1 insight
The interesting thesis is not “AI automates claims.”

It is:

> Commercial claims are becoming too data-rich for human adjusters to manually reconstruct, but insurers cannot remove human judgment from high-severity decisions. The winning architecture may therefore automate evidence reconstruction while routing only judgment-heavy exceptions to humans.

This creates the sharper product question:

> Does ClaimPilot own the evidence-reconstruction layer, or is it merely another claims assistant?

## Pass 2 opening
**Slide 1:** Commercial claims have become data-processing problems disguised as adjuster work.

**Slide 2:** An adjuster spends most of the first six hours reconstructing what happened—not deciding what to do.

**Slide 3:** ClaimPilot turns hundreds of documents and images into one reviewable claim record.

**Slide 4:** The adjuster reviews exceptions; the agent handles reconstruction.

## Pass 3 hostile question
> If Guidewire adds this functionality, what does ClaimPilot still own?

If the answer is merely “our AI is better,” the story is weak.

If the company can demonstrate proprietary claim-evidence graphs, embedded workflows, insurer-specific feedback loops, and faster deployment across fragmented legacy stacks, there may be a stronger structural advantage.

## Lesson
The storytelling process surfaced a strategy question. It did not merely create prettier slides.
